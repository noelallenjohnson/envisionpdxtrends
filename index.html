How a city funds housing creation

Housing production is a flow; thus, ratios and rates are the measures.

Homes are net new homes in completed projects, counted in the year the project was finished. Population: U.S. Census (2000, 2010, 2020) and the Portland State University estimate for 2025. Households: Census 2000 and 2010, and a 2026 private estimate (Esri). A check on the count: the Census reports 28,201 more housing units in Portland in 2010 than in 2000; whereas this dataset finds net new homes completed over the same years. Large-project housing production is now entirely subsidized

Institutional-scale projects are funded either by private investors or by public subsidy. Splitting them shows market rate investment forces historically power housing production.

Subsidized means a project receiving state, local bond or federal tax-credit money, or university housing. A market-rate project that includes required inclusionary units is counted as market rate because it receives no public money: the below-market rents are paid for inside the building, by its market-rate renters. When a rule requires some rents to be below market and does not offset the cost, it subsidizes some residents through others. Economists describe such a requirement as a tax on new housing paired with a price ceiling on the required homes (Ellickson, "The Irony of 'Inclusionary' Zoning," 1981). Oregon law now reflects this: Senate Bill 1521, signed in March 2026, requires Portland-area cities with an inclusionary mandate to offset its cost in full through payments, tax exemptions or fee waivers (Sightline Institute summary). Tags for the largest and most recent projects were checked one by one; older and smaller projects rely on matching to public affordable-housing records.

National recessions reset housing production flows

The city's two approval systems, land use and building permit, decide when larger buildings can proceed. Slower approvals can be seen to align with smaller flows, especially when the flow is cut off by a national recession. The math is simple: the longer approval takes, the shorter the window of time in which market-rate-funded production can occur.

Cases for 2010 to mid-2020 were transcribed by hand from city records; later cases were read from the written decisions. Cases before 2010 were read by script from the city's short case descriptions and are provisional. A case is linked to a building permit when the decision names the permit or when a permit of similar size was filed on the same site; some real links are missed, so the share lost is an upper estimate. Approvals from the last three years may still proceed.

Before and after the 2017 inclusionary housing requirement

Portland adopted an inclusionary housing requirement that took effect on February 1, 2017. For buildings of 20 or more homes, a fee, or below-market rents on a share of the homes was imposed; this tax being paid by the building's market rate renters (Ellickson, 1981). Applications on file before the deadline exempted a project, so vesting was an option in the sense finance theory uses the word: the right, without the obligation, to build later under the old rules. An option is worth paying for when the future is uncertain (Titman, "Urban Land Prices Under Uncertainty," 1985), which explains the rush to file; the record suggests this one often went unexercised. From February 2018 through 2019, homes permitted in projects of that size ran about 22% below the pace seen from 2014 to 2017.

The 20-home line is the rule's own threshold and does not follow the slider above. About this data

Sources

The land use and permit figures shown here come from the City of Portland's own records. The city has two approval steps for housing - land use approvals, where a project needs one, and building permit approvals. All counts are pulled from the city's official land use decisions - via City Auditor's Efiles archives. For building permit approvals, the City of Portland's public permit records are used via PortlandMaps. Distinction between market rate versus public/subsidized housing production comes from subsidy records of the Oregon Affordable Housing Inventory, Metro's Regulated Affordable Housing Inventory, the Portland Housing Bureau and Oregon Housing and Community Services - cross referenced to the specific building permit for each project. Every project counted here can be reviewed in the project list.

What counts as a new home

New construction, conversions of offices and hotels into housing, and homes added inside existing buildings such as accessory dwelling units. Remodels, hotels and replacements do not count. Demos that removed a home to build a new home in its place do not count as adding housing.

Projects

Because townhouses and subdivisions are approved as one project, but each home holds its own building permit, a 40 meters proximity and 270 days relationship was used to distinguish a discrete development. For example, a 14-home townhouse project counts once, at its real size (instead of as 14 specific, one unit development efforts.)

Sources of capital

Projects of 1 to 4 homes are typically funded from households with sufficient wealth to do so. Smaller development projects - from 5 homes up to the institutional thresholds - are typically funded by local and regional developers and investor groups. Larger projects at or above the minimum investment scale thresholds, be they rental or for sale, are those funded by institutional investment (or via public capital (i.e. city taxes, regional bond measures, federal low income housing tax credits, etc.))

Projection

The lighter bars for 2027 and 2028 count only projects already filed and awaiting a permit. They assume household-funded building continues at its recent pace, that between a quarter and 60% of pending mid-sized homes actually manifest into permitted new housing production, that most pending subsidized large projects proceed (60% to 90%), and that few pending market-rate large projects do (none to 25%). Projects not yet filed are not included.

Limits

Affordable-housing inventories lag by one to three years. Grouping by distance can occasionally join deals or split phases. Of about 17,000 projects, around 550 were reviewed individually; the rest follow the rules above. Where this site applies its own rules (grouping permits into projects, subtracting demolitions, tagging subsidy, and projecting 2027 and 2028), those are choices made here and not city figures. Each is described above, and every project can be checked in the project list.

About this housing data visualization project

Envision Portland Trends began in the summer of 2020 as a project to visualize the city's own housing data, built as applied learning in housing production, city governance and statistics / computer science realms. The effort was funded by Old i, LLC via federal COVID PPP stimulus to create socially helpful learning for Williams College students that also can enable better civic decision making. It is not a study. It is a mirror: the City of Portland's own land use and building permit records, organized so that anyone can see the patterns for themselves. Where the site interprets a pattern, it says so and cites its source.
